External Negotiations and Customs Union Stability

Working paper · International trade and political economy

Why can a customs union remain intact while repeatedly struggling to approve major external agreements? I separate two margins that are often conflated: the value of membership relative to a unilateral outside option, and the approval of a proposed bloc agreement relative to the status quo. I study these margins in a structural model with reciprocal external bargaining and asymmetric applied tariffs, calibrated to five benchmark years from 2004 to 2017.

In the focal China and United States exercises, Uruguay gains more by bargaining through Mercosur than by bargaining alone, while Brazil and Argentina obtain smaller or negative aggregate effects and face concentrated sectoral losses. The results show how bloc bargaining can make membership valuable while unanimity and uneven incidence create approval gridlock.

Politics and Public Banks: BNDES Loans to Local Governments in Brazil

Economía LACEA Journal (2026, Vol. 1)

Guilherme Paiva Pinto, Mauricio S. Bugarin, and Rodrigo Schneider

Selected presentations: Brazilian Econometric Society Meeting (SBE 2025), Econometric Society European Winter Meeting (EWMES 2025), Latin American Law and Economics (LAWLE 2024)

This study advances a quantitative political economy analysis of development banking within federative systems, focusing on Brazil's National Bank for Economic and Social Development (BNDES). Moving beyond traditional fiscal transfer models, we investigate the mechanisms through which federal credit operations are allocated to municipal governments, emphasizing the political incentives that influence these disbursements.

We adapt the Strategic Partisan Transfers Hypothesis (SPTH) to the development banking context, formalizing the interaction between federal, state, and local political alignments within Brazil's regulatory structure.

Tariff Cuts, External Shocks, and Strategic Inputs: The EU-Mercosur Agreement in a Changing World

Working paper · Quantitative trade

After more than two decades of negotiations, the EU–Mercosur agreements were signed in January 2026 and the interim trade agreement began provisional application in May 2026. This paper asks how the economic effects of the same goods-tariff schedule changed during that delay. I code Annex 2-A and evaluate it in 2007, 2011, 2014, 2017, and 2022 using a six-region, 49-sector quantitative trade model.

Tariff-only aggregate effects are small. The central result is sectoral exposure: manufacturing losses in Argentina and Brazil remain concentrated, EU agriculture remains politically exposed, and imports shift toward the European Union. U.S. tariff-risk and rare-earth scenarios show that external insurance and strategic inputs can matter more than the legal tariff component alone.

How I Teach Economics

I organize class around a repeatable sequence: begin with a concrete question, make assumptions explicit, derive the model’s implications, compare those implications with data and graphs, and then discuss trade-offs, limitations, and policy. This structure helps students see economic models as tools for disciplined reasoning rather than answers to memorize.

My courses use frequent low-stakes practice—including short problem drills, polls, graph interpretation, and brief writing—alongside presentations and structured debates. These activities give students early feedback and repeated practice turning evidence into clear, testable economic arguments.
