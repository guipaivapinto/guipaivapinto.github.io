Wakanda: choices and opportunity cost

Models · Fictional classroom economy

Wakanda is our fictional example of an economy with limited resources. Its production possibilities frontier (PPF) shows the combinations of goods it can produce. Producing more of one good means giving up some of the other.

Reading the graph

Points on the frontier use resources efficiently. Points inside it leave some capacity unused.

The amount given up is the opportunity cost. An outward shift means the economy can produce more.

Main idea: Comparative advantage means producing a good at a lower opportunity cost.

Practice (optional)

An economy moves along an unchanged PPF to produce more of one good. What happens to the other good on a downward-sloping frontier?

  1. Its output falls.
  2. Its output must also rise.
  3. Its output stays fixed.
  4. Its price must fall.
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Its output falls.

On a downward-sloping frontier, producing more of one good requires producing less of the other.

The Miracle of Bern

Models · 1954

West Germany beat Hungary in the 1954 World Cup final in Bern. We used that surprising result to discuss predictions: a model can be useful even when events turn out differently from what people expected.

More about this example

The photographs in the slides show the setting. The lesson is to ask what a prediction assumed and how certain it was.

Key dates
  1. 1954 — West Germany and Hungary play the final in Bern, Switzerland.

Main idea: A useful model helps explain a problem while leaving out some details.

Practice (optional)

What should we do when an outcome differs from a prediction?

  1. Every model is useless.
  2. Check the prediction’s assumptions and how uncertain it was.
  3. The winning outcome was certain in advance.
  4. One event establishes a universal economic law.
Show answer and explanation

Answer: B. Check the prediction’s assumptions and how uncertain it was.

An unexpected result gives us a reason to check the assumptions and uncertainty behind a prediction.

South Korea: crisis and recovery

Growth · 1997–98; long-run comparison 1960–2024

South Korea’s economy contracted during the 1997–98 financial crisis. The gold-collection campaign was one part of the response. Foreign debt, problems obtaining funds, and changes in banks and businesses also mattered.

Reading the graph

Find the downturn in real GDP per person, then follow the recovery.

The longer line shows growth over decades. It does not tell us how much of the recovery came from the gold campaign.

Key dates
  1. 1997 — Foreign-currency debt and difficulty obtaining funds deepen the crisis.
  2. 1998 — People donate gold during the wider effort to address the crisis.
  3. Long run — GDP per person rises over decades, despite recessions.

Main idea: Recovery brings output back after a downturn. Long-run growth raises what the economy can produce over time.

Practice (optional)

Output falls sharply and then returns to its previous level. What does that return show most directly?

  1. A permanent doubling of productive capacity.
  2. The end of all future recessions.
  3. Recovery from the contraction.
  4. A change in the definition of GDP.
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. Recovery from the contraction.

Returning to the earlier output level shows recovery. Sustained growth requires output to keep rising over a longer period.

U.S. downturns and stabilization policy

Output & prices · Great Depression; postwar policy; Volcker disinflation

The Great Depression and the fight against inflation under Paul Volcker show two problems for economic policy. A recession can leave workers and equipment idle. Policies that bring inflation down can also reduce spending and employment in the short run.

More about this example

Use the dates to distinguish the Depression from the later period of disinflation. Each had different economic conditions and policy goals.

Key dates
  1. 1929–33 — Output falls sharply and banks fail during the Great Depression.
  2. 1934–48 — Marriner Eccles leads the Federal Reserve.
  3. 1979–87 — Paul Volcker leads the Federal Reserve as it works to reduce inflation.

Main idea: Stabilization policy aims to limit swings in output, employment, and inflation.

Practice (optional)

Why is the Volcker episode relevant to stabilization policy?

  1. Reducing inflation can involve short-run output and employment costs.
  2. A fall in inflation necessarily means all prices fall.
  3. Interest rates determine GDP by definition.
  4. Disinflation eliminates structural unemployment.
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. Reducing inflation can involve short-run output and employment costs.

Bringing inflation down can reduce spending and employment for a time. Disinflation means prices rise more slowly; they may still be rising.

Denmark: pharmaceuticals and GDP

Output & prices · 2019–2025 saved GDP series; pharmaceutical expansion

Growth in the pharmaceutical industry helped Denmark’s economy grow. This example shows how a large industry can affect national GDP. It also raises a question about where production is counted when companies operate across borders.

Reading the graph

Compare the GDP line with the contribution from pharmaceuticals.

An industry’s contribution to growth tells us how much it adds to the change in GDP. Its share of GDP tells us how large it is.

Key dates
  1. 2019–2025 — The graph follows GDP and growth in the pharmaceutical industry.
  2. November 2023 — Novo Nordisk announces a major factory expansion in Kalundborg.

Main idea: GDP counts the value added through production.

Practice (optional)

A pharmaceutical company’s share price doubles. What can we conclude about Denmark’s GDP from that fact alone?

  1. GDP doubles.
  2. Pharmaceutical production doubles.
  3. The GDP effect cannot be determined from the share price alone.
  4. Every Danish household’s income doubles.
Show answer and explanation

Answer: C. The GDP effect cannot be determined from the share price alone.

A higher share price tells us what investors will pay. We need production data to calculate the effect on GDP.

GDP, the CPI, and inflation

Output & prices · Use dates and base years in each saved series

Nominal GDP uses current prices. Real GDP adjusts for price changes so we can study changes in production. The consumer price index (CPI) follows consumer purchases; the GDP deflator covers final goods and services produced within the country.

More about this example

Inflation is the percentage change in a price index between two dates.

For example, an index rising from 100 to 105 means prices rose by 5%. Check that the series use the same dates and adjustment method.

Main idea: Use real GDP to study production and the CPI to study consumer prices.

Practice (optional)

A price index rises from 100 to 105. What is inflation over that interval?

  1. 5%.
  2. 105%.
  3. 0.05%.
  4. An increase of 5 percentage points in the unemployment rate.
Show answer and explanation

Answer: A. 5%.

(105 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = 5%.

Natural resources and sustainable growth

Sustainability · Malthus 1798; later resource-limit warnings

Malthus predicted that population growth would put pressure on limited land and keep living standards low. Technological progress helped production per worker rise instead. Later concerns about coal and oil raised a similar question: can growth continue with limited resources?

More about this example

Higher resource prices encourage people to use less, improve efficiency, and find substitutes.

These responses can ease shortages. Environmental damage may still need policy action when prices do not reflect its costs.

Key dates
  1. 1798 — Malthus writes about population growth and limited land.
  2. 1865 — Jevons writes about coal supplies and extraction costs.
  3. Later growth — Technology and investment help raise production per worker.

Main idea: Technology and price incentives help economies adapt to scarce resources.

Practice (optional)

  2. Automatic elimination of all pollution.
  3. An unchanged incentive to use the resource.
  4. Conservation, efficiency, and development of substitutes.
Show answer and explanation

Answer: D. Conservation, efficiency, and development of substitutes.

A higher price rewards saving the resource and finding alternatives. It does not automatically solve pollution.
