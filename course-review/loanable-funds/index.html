Interest rates, investment, and loanable funds

Is a dollar next year worth a dollar today? Change the rates below, then connect one firm's decision to the market for loanable funds.

All examples are hypothetical. Dollars are measured in constant purchasing power, and rates are annual real rates. Cash flows are assumed certain to isolate the role of interest rates.

1. Present Value: What Is Future Money Worth Today?

Suppose you will receive $1,000 in the future. Change the amount, interest rate, and years to see what that payment is worth today.

PV = FV / (1 + r)n

FV = future value · r = annual interest rate as a decimal · n = years until payment

Year 0 means payment today, so PV equals FV. At 0% interest, PV equals FV at every year. The payment amount is the same across years; this graph compares alternative payment dates, not a stream of annual payments.

Apply it to an investment decision

At 10%, $100 today grows to $110 in one year. That means $110 next year and $100 today have the same value at that rate.

A machine costs $900 today and pays $1,000 once at the end of the selected period. What is that payment worth today?

$1,000 / (1.05)¹ = $952.38

Net present value = $952.38 − $900 = $52.38

Invest: the payment's present value exceeds the cost.

FV = future payment; r = rate written as a decimal; n = years. A positive NPV adds value. A zero NPV is break-even.

Check the opportunity cost

The machine's discount rate represents the return available on a comparable alternative. Using the firm's own savings does not make capital free. In actual decisions, the discount rate must also reflect risk and financing conditions. Match nominal cash flows with nominal rates, or real cash flows with real rates.

2. Why does demand for loanable funds slope downward?

A lower real interest rate makes more projects worth undertaking. Firms want more funds for investment. This is a movement along demand, not a shift of demand.
