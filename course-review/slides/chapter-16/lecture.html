Seigniorage and the Inflation Tax

M: state-issued money stock · ΔM: new money issued · P: price level

Seigniorage

Nominal revenue: ΔM = Mₜ − Mₜ₋₁. Real revenue: ΔM / Pₜ.

The inflation tax

Rising prices reduce the purchasing power of money held by the public.

Government finance

Money issuance can finance spending, while the resulting inflation imposes a cost on money holders.

IMF, Suriname: Toward Stability and Growth (2009), Box 5.2

The Logic of Hyperinflation

Money-financed deficits

The government relies on money creation to cover a persistent financing gap.

Avoiding the inflation tax

As money loses purchasing power, people hold fewer real domestic money balances.

A reinforcing process

If the government issues money faster to obtain the same resources, inflation and avoidance can reinforce each other.

Nachega, IMF Working Paper 05/221 (2005)

FunctionDuring hyperinflation
Store of valuePeople may save in dollars, gold, or goods to limit losses on domestic money.
Unit of accountPrices and contracts may be quoted in a more stable currency or indexed.

Moderate Inflation and Disinflation

The political business cycle

Elected leaders may seek expansion before elections to raise output and reduce unemployment.

The cost of disinflation

Contractionary monetary or fiscal policy reduces demand and can temporarily raise unemployment.

The political dilemma

Tolerating inflation may postpone the unpopular loss of output and jobs associated with reducing it.

Keller and May (1984), The Presidential Political Business Cycle of 1972 · Federal Reserve Board, Waller (2023), The Unstable Phillips Curve

Potential Output and Unemployment

Potential output, Yᴾ

The real GDP the economy can sustain with resources used at normal rates.

The output gap

100 × (actual real GDP − potential real GDP) / potential real GDP

The unemployment rate

Actual unemployment = natural unemployment + cyclical unemployment.

Okun’s Law

Output gaps and unemployment deviations from the natural rate move in opposite directions.

OutputOutput gapUnemploymentCyclical unemployment
Actual = potentialZeroActual = naturalZero
Actual > potentialPositiveActual < naturalNegative
Actual < potentialNegativeActual > naturalPositive

Output gap (%) ≈ −2 × (u − u*)

u*: natural unemployment rate. u − u*: cyclical unemployment, in percentage points. The coefficient is an approximate US rule of thumb.

The Short-Run Phillips Curve

New Zealand economist A.W.H. Phillips (1958): UK unemployment and nominal wage growth, 1861–1957. Here: consumer-price inflation.

What the curve shows

A downward relationship between inflation and unemployment, with expectations and supply conditions fixed.

A → B: demand restraint

Weaker labor demand limits wage increases. Inflation falls, while unemployment rises.

B → A: demand expansion

A tighter labor market strengthens wage bargaining and adds price pressure; unemployment falls.

Reserve Bank of New Zealand, Monetary policy objectives (2019) · Federal Reserve Board, Waller (2023), The Unstable Phillips Curve

Supply Shocks and the Phillips Curve

A: before an oil shock

Unemployment is 5% and inflation is 4% on SRPC₀.

Higher oil costs: an upward shift

Oil costs raise inflation at each unemployment rate. Falling costs reverse the shift.

B: inflation and unemployment rise

Firms reduce output and hiring. Workers may seek wage increases to restore lost purchasing power.

Oil Shocks and Stagflation

1973–1975: after the 1973–74 oil embargo

US inflation: 6.3% → 9.1%; unemployment: 4.9% → 8.5%.

1978–1980: the Iranian revolution and second oil shock

US inflation: 7.6% → 13.5%; unemployment: 6.1% → 7.2%.

Stagflation

A supply shift can raise inflation and unemployment together while output weakens.

From the short-run trade-off to its long-run limit: what changes when people come to expect inflation?

Inflation Expectations and the Short-Run Phillips Curve

Expected inflation

The inflation rate employers and workers expect in the near future. Initially, actual and expected inflation are both 2%.

Friedman and Phelps

Expectations about future inflation affect wage bargaining and price setting today. Expected inflation rises to 4%.

An upward shift

At 5% unemployment, inflation is now 4% rather than 2%. A higher expected rate shifts SRPC upward.

Adjustment to the Long Run

A: initial long-run equilibrium

Unemployment is 5%; actual and expected inflation are 2%.

B: short-run demand expansion

Inflation rises to 4% before expectations adjust. Unemployment temporarily falls to 3%.

C: expectations catch up

Wages and prices incorporate 4% inflation. The short-run curve shifts up; unemployment returns to 5%.

The Long-Run Phillips Curve and NAIRU

A vertical long-run curve

Stable inflation of 2% or 4% has the same u* = 5%. Higher fully expected inflation cannot permanently lower unemployment.

NAIRU

Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment: the natural rate in this model, consistent with stable inflation, other conditions fixed.

Inflation pressure

Below u*, inflation tends to rise; above it, to fall. At u*, cyclical unemployment is zero; frictional and structural unemployment remain.

RBA, The Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment · Federal Reserve Board, Mishkin (2007), Estimating Potential Output · Federal Reserve Board, Waller (2023), The Unstable Phillips Curve
