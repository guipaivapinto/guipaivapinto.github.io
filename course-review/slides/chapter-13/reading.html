Fiscal Policy and Stabilization

Definition

Government decisions about purchases, taxes, and transfers.

Stabilization goal

Support output and employment in a recession; restrain excess demand in a boom.

Policy direction

Expansionary policy raises AD; contractionary policy reduces AD.

Public services and redistribution are also fiscal goals.

Fiscal Policy and Aggregate Expenditure

Y = C + I + G + NX

C: Consumption

Household taxes and transfers change disposable income.

Tax cuts or higher transfers can raise C.

I: Private investment

Business tax incentives and expected sales affect investment.

Borrowing can also raise financing costs and weaken I.

G: Government purchases

Government buys current goods and services.

The direct expenditure channel.

NX: Net exports

Higher domestic spending can increase imports.

Imports reduce NX, other things equal.

Expansionary Fiscal Policy

Starting output = 90 · Potential output = 100

Expansionary · AD increases

Recessionary gap

Output is below potential.

Policy change

Government purchases increase.

Demand response

AD shifts right; production rises.

New equilibrium

Output reaches 100; the price level rises.

Expansionary alternatives: G ↑, household T ↓, or TR ↑.

Contractionary Fiscal Policy

Starting output = 110 · Potential output = 100

Contractionary · AD decreases

Inflationary gap

Output is above potential.

Policy change

Government purchases decrease.

Demand response

AD shifts left; production falls.

New equilibrium

Output returns to 100; the price level falls.

Contractionary alternatives: G ↓, household T ↑, or TR ↓.

Three Criticisms of Fiscal Expansion

CriticismMechanismQualification
Investment crowding out
Borrowing can raise interest rates and reduce I.
The size depends on economic and monetary conditions.
Consumption offset
Current tax financing can reduce disposable income and C.
The reduction need not cancel the extra G.
Ricardian equivalence
A tax cut may be saved for expected future taxes.
A conditional tax-timing argument, holding spending fixed.

An offset can weaken a policy without eliminating its effect.

References: CBO fiscal-policy framework; CRS fiscal-policy review

The Spending Multiplier

The first payment becomes income

A government purchase pays a worker or supplier.

Some income is spent again

Recipients consume part and save part of the extra income.

Further rounds become smaller

New consumption creates more income; saving limits the chain.

The output change combines the first spending change and the later rounds.

A Household Tax Cut

Household taxes fall by $100 billion · MPC = 0.8

Disposable income rises

ΔT = −$100 billion; ΔYD = +$100 billion.

Households consume part

Initially, consumption rises $80 billion and saving rises $20 billion.

Later rounds add to output

ΔY = $80 billion × 5 = +$400 billion.

The tax multiplier has a negative sign

kT = −4, so ΔY = (−4) × (−$100 billion) = +$400 billion.

Income Taxes and the Multiplier

MPC = 0.8 · Income-tax rate t = 25% · Fixed price level

Additional output

ΔY = $100

→

Taxes and disposable income

ΔT = $25   ΔYD = $75

→

Additional consumption

ΔC = 0.8 × $75 = $60

Only $60 reaches the next spending round, compared with $80 under fixed taxes.

MPC remains 0.8; MPS remains 0.2.

kG = 1 / [1 − MPC(1 − t)]

kG = 1 / [1 − 0.8(0.75)] = 2.5, compared with 5 under fixed taxes.

Automatic Stabilizers

Income falls

Tax payments fall under existing income-tax rules.

Unemployment rises

More eligible workers receive existing benefits.

Consumption is cushioned

Disposable income falls by less than it otherwise would.

No new law is needed for these responses.
