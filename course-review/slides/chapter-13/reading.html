Fiscal Policy and Stabilization

Definition

Fiscal policy uses government purchases, taxes, and transfers to influence aggregate demand.

How it works

Purchases change G directly; household taxes and transfers change disposable income and C.

Stabilization goal

Support output and employment in a downturn; restrain excess demand and inflation pressure.

Expansionary policy shifts AD right; contractionary policy shifts AD left.

Fiscal Policy and Aggregate Expenditure

Y = C + I + G + NX

C: Consumption

Household taxes and transfers change disposable income.

Tax cuts or higher transfers can raise C.

I: Private investment

Business tax incentives and expected sales affect investment.

Borrowing can also raise financing costs and weaken I.

G: Government purchases

Government buys current goods and services.

The direct expenditure channel.

NX: Net exports

Higher domestic spending can increase imports.

Imports reduce NX, other things equal.

Government Purchases and Tax Cuts

Is there a difference between increasing G and reducing T by the same amount?

Yes. Their multiplier effects differ.

Potential Output and Policy Tradeoffs

Productive capacity changes

LRAS shifts; SRAS may also shift. Adjustment is toward the new potential output.

Choose the relevant benchmark

Fiscal choices depend on the remaining gap and the policy objective. Restoring the old GDP level may be inappropriate.

Keep the two mechanisms distinct

Wage and cost adjustment shifts SRAS; fiscal stabilization usually shifts AD.

Results depend on timing, financing, household spending, productivity, and central-bank responses.

ECON_OX 112 · Chapter 13Guilherme Paiva Pinto26 / 39

The limits of continued fiscal expansion

As spare capacity disappears, additional demand generates smaller output gains and stronger inflation pressure.

Substantial spare capacity

Firms employ idle resources. Output rises considerably, with limited price pressure.

Capacity becomes tighter

Overtime, scarce workers, and production bottlenecks raise costs. Output responds less and prices respond more.

Continued expansion

Persistent demand growth beyond productive capacity strengthens inflation pressure. Higher interest rates may further reduce the output response.

Fiscal expansion becomes less effective at increasing real output as supply constraints tighten.

The Risks of a Large Public Debt

Crowding out: Government borrowing can raise financing costs and reduce private investment.

Financial pressure: Interest payments leave less budget room for services and investment.

Default risk: The government may fail to honor its promised payment terms.

The Limits of Monetary Financing

Real resources: New money does not itself supply workers, machinery, or goods.

Inflation risk: Persistent financing beyond productive capacity erodes purchasing power and can raise borrowing costs.

Currency limits: Greece could not issue euros independently; foreign-currency debt still requires foreign currency.

Sources: IMF, sovereign debt · ECB, monetary-financing limits
