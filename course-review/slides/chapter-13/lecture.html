Fiscal Policy and Stabilization

Definition

Fiscal policy uses government purchases, taxes, and transfers to influence aggregate demand.

How it works

Purchases change G directly; household taxes and transfers change disposable income and C.

Stabilization goal

Support output and employment in a downturn; restrain excess demand and inflation pressure.

Expansionary policy shifts AD right; contractionary policy shifts AD left.

Fiscal Policy and Aggregate Expenditure

Y = C + I + G + NX

C: Consumption

Household taxes and transfers change disposable income.

Tax cuts or higher transfers can raise C.

I: Private investment

Business tax incentives and expected sales affect investment.

Borrowing can also raise financing costs and weaken I.

G: Government purchases

Government buys current goods and services.

The direct expenditure channel.

NX: Net exports

Higher domestic spending can increase imports.

Imports reduce NX, other things equal.

Expansionary · AD increases

Recessionary gap

Output is below potential.

Policy change

Government purchases increase.

Demand response

AD shifts right; production rises.

New equilibrium

Output reaches 100; the price level rises.

Expansionary alternatives: G ↑, household T ↓, or TR ↑.

Three Criticisms of Fiscal Expansion

CriticismMechanismQualification
Investment crowding out
Borrowing can raise interest rates and reduce I.
The size depends on economic and monetary conditions.
Consumption offset
Current tax financing can reduce disposable income and C.
The reduction need not cancel the extra G.
Ricardian equivalence
A tax cut may be saved for expected future taxes.
A conditional tax-timing argument, holding spending fixed.

An offset can weaken a policy without eliminating its effect.

References: CBO fiscal-policy framework; CRS fiscal-policy review

The Spending-Round Multiplier

Start with initial expenditure

Identify the spending on current output caused directly by the policy.

Multiply the initial spending

With MPC = 0.8, the spending-round factor is m = 1/(1 − MPC) = 5.

Compare the total multiplier effect

Net output change: ΔY = initial expenditure change × m.

The same m can give different output effects when initial spending differs.

A Household Tax Cut

Household taxes fall by $100 billion · MPC = 0.8

Disposable income rises

ΔT = −$100 billion; ΔYD = +$100 billion.

Initial spending rises by $80 billion

With MPC = 0.8, households consume $80 billion and save $20 billion.

Apply the same spending-round factor m = 5

Net output effect: ΔY = $80 billion × 5 = +$400 billion.

Keep the denominator clear

m = 400/80 = 5; output per $1 of tax cut = 400/100 = 4.

Income Taxes and the Spending Multiplier

MPC = 0.8 · Income-tax rate t = 25% · Fixed price level

Additional output

ΔY = $100

→

Taxes and disposable income

ΔT = $25   ΔYD = $75

→

Additional consumption

ΔC = 0.8 × $75 = $60

Only $60 reaches the next spending round, compared with $80 under fixed taxes.

MPC remains 0.8; MPS remains 0.2.

m = 1 / [1 − MPC(1 − t)]

m = 1 / [1 − 0.8(0.75)] = 2.5, compared with 5 under fixed taxes.

Increase purchasesGovernment buys more current goods and services.AD rightOutput and price level rise.
Cut household taxesDisposable income rises; households spend part.AD rightOutput and price level rise.
Increase transfersRecipients spend part of the extra income.AD rightOutput and price level rise.

Potential Output and Policy Tradeoffs

Productive capacity changes

LRAS shifts; SRAS may also shift. Adjustment is toward the new potential output.

Choose the relevant benchmark

Fiscal choices depend on the remaining gap and the policy objective. Restoring the old GDP level may be inappropriate.

Keep the two mechanisms distinct

Wage and cost adjustment shifts SRAS; fiscal stabilization usually shifts AD.

Results depend on timing, financing, household spending, productivity, and central-bank responses.
