Factors That Shift Aggregate Demand

At the same price level · Other determinants unchanged

Households

Higher expected income or wealth can raise C.

Firms

Higher expected sales can raise I; idle capital can reduce I.

Policy

Higher G, lower taxes, or lower interest rates can support AD.

Higher planned C, I, G, or NX shifts AD right.

Short-Run Aggregate Supply

A bakery’s wage contract fixes hourly pay at $20 for the month

Some input costs adjust slowly

Nominal pay remains fixed temporarily.

Selling prices rise first

Extra production can become more profitable.

Output increases

The economy moves along an upward-sloping SRAS curve.

Sticky wages mean delayed adjustment.

Sticky Prices and Short-Run Output

Adjustment takes time

Contracts and repricing costs delay changes in wages and prices.

Fewer orders

Firms may cut production and hiring before changing prices.

Short-run consequence

Spending shocks can change real output as well as prices.

Short-Run Equilibrium: Demand and Supply Contractions

Above Pe: excess supply puts downward pressure on prices.

Below Pe: excess demand puts upward pressure on prices.

Demand contraction

Lower confidence reduces consumption.

AD shifts left: the old price is above the new Pₑ.

New equilibrium: Y falls; P falls.

Supply contraction

Higher oil prices raise production costs.

SRAS shifts left: the old price is below the new Pₑ.

New equilibrium: Y falls; P rises.

Output Gaps

One shock: consumption falls · Potential output remains 100

Initial equilibrium

Y = 100: output equals potential.

Demand falls

AD shifts left; SRAS and LRAS remain fixed.

Short-run equilibrium

Y = 90: a recessionary gap.

Measure the gap

(90 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = −10%.

Y < potential: recessionary gap. Y > potential: inflationary gap.

Appendix: AD and Income–Expenditure

At a given price level

The income–expenditure model finds output where AE = Y.

Compare a lower price level

Purchasing power and interest-sensitive spending can rise.

Trace aggregate demand

Higher planned spending produces a larger equilibrium Y.

Changing the price level traces AD; changing other demand influences shifts AD.

Worked Case: Higher Energy Costs

Initial Y = 100 · Initial P = 100 · Potential output = 100

Initial equilibrium

AD, SRAS, and LRAS meet at potential.

Initial shock

Energy costs rise; SRAS shifts left.

Short-run equilibrium

Y = 90 and P = 110: output falls while prices rise.

Later adjustment

Energy costs return to their initial level; SRAS shifts back.

Final equilibrium

Y = 100 and P = 100: the initial equilibrium is restored.

Identify the shock, shift one curve, locate equilibrium, then explain adjustment.
