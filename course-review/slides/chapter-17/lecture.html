    Potential Output and the Output Gap

    Actual real GDPPotential real GDP
    950 billion dollars1,000 billion dollars
    • Potential output, Yᴾ: the real GDP the economy can sustain with resources used at normal rates.
    • Output gap (%) = 100 × (actual real GDP − potential real GDP) / potential real GDP.
    • A recessionary gap means actual real GDP is below potential real GDP.

    Output gap = 100 × (950 − 1,000) / 1,000 = −5%.

    Fiscal Stabilization

    Increase in government purchasesSpending multiplier
    30 billion dollars2
    • Higher government purchases increase planned spending.
    • The multiplier links a spending change to a larger change in equilibrium real GDP.
    • Near potential output, stronger demand can put more pressure on prices.

    Change in equilibrium real GDP = 2 × 30 = 60 billion dollars.

    Demand and Supply Shocks

    • Case A: households cut spending; production costs stay fixed.
    • Case B: oil costs rise; aggregate demand stays fixed.
    • For each case, select the curve that shifts and the short-run output direction.

    A: AD shifts left; real GDP falls. B: SRAS shifts left; real GDP falls.

    Expansionary demand policy can close A's gap. In B, it can support output while adding price pressure.

    Money Growth and Inflation

    Money growthReal GDP growthVelocity
    8% per year3% per yearConstant
    • With constant velocity, money growth approximately equals inflation plus real GDP growth.
    • Inflation ≈ money growth − real GDP growth.
    • This is a conditional long-run relationship.

    Approximate inflation = 8% − 3% = 5% per year.

    The Long-Run Phillips Curve

    • Stronger demand can temporarily reduce unemployment below the natural rate.
    • As inflation expectations adjust, unemployment returns to the natural rate.
    • The long-run Phillips curve is vertical at NAIRU; keeping unemployment below it requires continuing increases in inflation.

    The Lower Bound on Interest Rates

    • Near-zero short-term nominal interest rates limit further conventional rate cuts.
    • A low nominal rate can still be a high real rate when expected inflation is low or negative.
    • Asset purchases, forward guidance, and fiscal policy remain possible tools.

      • Identify the shock before choosing a policy.
      • Check the output gap, inflation expectations, and policy constraints.
      • Separate support for spending from changes in productive capacity.
      • Timing alone does not identify a policy’s separate effect when other shocks occur.
