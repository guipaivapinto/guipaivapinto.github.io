Minecraft: Game or Company?

Assume direct purchases by U.S. residents from Swedish residents; $30 is illustrative.

Did the $2.5 billion acquisition count as a $2.5 billion import?

PurchaseWhat the buyer receivesWhere the purchase is recorded
$30 for a Minecraft downloadA license to use the gameCurrent account: imported computer service
$2.5 billion for ownership of MojangOwnership of a business and a claim on future earningsFinancial account: foreign direct investment

Current account (CA): records trade, income and transfers between countries. The $30 download is a service import, lowering the U.S. trade balance by $30.

Financial account (FA): records investment and borrowing between countries. Buying Mojang transfers business ownership; the $2.5 billion is a financial transaction, not an import.

Capital Flows and the Balance of Payments

  • The balance of payments records transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world.
  • The current account, CA, records trade in goods and services, factor income, and transfers.
  • Capital flows arise from asset transactions. Here, the financial account, FA, is capital inflow minus capital outflow.

Current and Financial Account Identities

Here, FA = net capital inflow. Set aside the capital account and statistical discrepancy.

CA⁺ + FA⁺ = CA⁻ + FA⁻

Positive CA and FA entries equal the magnitudes of negative CA and FA entries.

(CA⁺ − CA⁻) + (FA⁺ − FA⁻) = 0

Subtract negative-entry totals to obtain each account's net balance.

CA + FA = 0

The two net balances offset each other.

CA = −FA

A current-account deficit corresponds to a net financial inflow.

The Current Account, Saving, and Investment

Assume net factor income and net transfers are zero, so CA = NX. S denotes national saving.

Set aside the capital account and statistical discrepancy.

Y = C + I + G + X − IM

Output equals consumption, investment, government purchases, and net exports.

S = Y − C − G

Income not used for consumption or government purchases is national saving.

S − I = X − IM = CA

Saving above domestic investment corresponds to a current-account surplus.

FA = I − S = −CA

Investment above national saving requires net financial inflow.

Modelling the Financial Account

  • With comparable risk and open capital markets, funds move toward the higher real interest rate.
  • Country A: S > I, so funds flow out and FA < 0.
  • Country B: I > S, so funds flow in and FA > 0.

Determinants and Two-Way Capital Flows

  • Saving differs with household choices, demographics, and government budgets.
  • Investment opportunities differ with productivity and expected profits; risk, institutions, and restrictions also matter.
  • Two-way capital flows: residents buy foreign assets while foreigners buy domestic assets.
  • Diversification and international business generate flows in both directions. FA records the difference between inflows and outflows.

Understanding Exchange Rates

  • International purchases may require payment in the seller's currency.
  • The foreign exchange market lets buyers exchange one currency for another.
  • An exchange rate is the price of one currency in terms of another.

For euros, e = euros per U.S. dollar = 1 / (dollars per euro).

€90 ÷ US$100 = €0.90 per US$1.
US$20 × 150 yen/US$1 = 3,000 yen.

Quantities of U.S. dollars per period. D₁ is higher demand.

  • Dollar demand slopes down: a higher €/US$1 makes U.S. goods and assets costlier in euros.
  • Dollar supply slopes up in this model: at a higher €/US$1, residents choose to offer more dollars.
  • Equilibrium: at €0.80 per US$1, dollars demanded = dollars supplied = 100.
  • Higher dollar demand: D₀ shifts right to D₁; e rises to 1.00 and the dollar appreciates.

Changes in the Dollar Market

Each case starts from the same initial equilibrium.

Initial equilibrium

D₀ and S₀ meet at €0.80 per US$1 and Q = 100.

Dollar demand rises

D shifts right: e rises to 1.00, Q rises to 120; the dollar appreciates.

Dollar demand falls

D shifts left: e falls to 0.60, Q falls to 80; the dollar depreciates.

Dollar supply rises

S shifts right: e falls to 0.60, Q rises to 120; the dollar depreciates.

Dollar supply falls

S shifts left: e rises to 1.00, Q falls to 80; the dollar appreciates.

Determinants of Dollar Demand

  • U.S. goods and services: stronger euro-area demand for U.S. products shifts dollar demand right.
  • Relative asset returns: more attractive expected returns on U.S. assets, at comparable risk, raise dollar demand.
  • Exchange-rate expectations: expected dollar appreciation can increase demand for dollars today.
  • Dollar supply: stronger U.S. demand for euro-area goods or assets shifts the supply of dollars right.

Nominal and Real Exchange Rates

P_US and P_EU are comparable basket prices in dollars and euros.

e = €0.90 per US$1

The nominal exchange rate is the price of dollars in euros.

q = e × P_US / P_EU

The real exchange rate compares basket prices after currency conversion.

q = 0.90 × 100 / 100 = 0.90

A US$100 basket costs €90; the euro-area basket costs €100.

Inflation and Exchange Rates

Suppose U.S. inflation is 10%: the basket rises from US$100 to US$110.

Euro-area inflation is 0%: its basket stays at €100.

Unchanged e: q = 0.90 × 110 / 100 = 0.99

The U.S. basket now costs €99. Higher relative inflation causes real dollar appreciation.

Offsetting depreciation: e = 90 / 110 ≈ €0.8182 per US$1

The U.S. basket costs €90 again, so q returns to 0.90. This is the rate needed to keep the relative price unchanged.
