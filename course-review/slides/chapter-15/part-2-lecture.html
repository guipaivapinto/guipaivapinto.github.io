A Standard Taylor Rule

i = r* + π + 0.5(π − π*) + 0.5g

•i: nominal policy-rate benchmark; r*: neutral real rate.

•π: inflation; π*: inflation goal; g: output gap in percent.

•Higher inflation relative to the goal or a larger output gap raises the benchmark, holding other inputs fixed.

Illustrative 1993-style rule. Rates and gaps enter as percentage numbers, such as 4 and −2.

Calculating a Policy-Rate Benchmark

Fixed illustrative inputValue
Neutral real rate r*2%
Inflation goal π*2%
Current inflation π4%
Output gap g−2%

•Baseline term: r* + π = 2 + 4 = 6.

•Inflation response: 0.5 × (4 − 2) = +1.

•Output response: 0.5 × (−2) = −1.

•Benchmark: i = 6 + 1 − 1 = 6%.

Why the Rule Responds to Inflation

Δi = 1.5 × Δπ (holding r*, π*, and g fixed)

•One extra percentage point of inflation adds 1 point through π and 0.5 point through the inflation-gap term.

•The nominal rate rises by 1.5 percentage points.

•If expected inflation rises by 1 point too, the approximate real rate rises by 0.5 point.

Inflation Targeting

•An inflation-targeting framework states an inflation objective and uses forecasts, communication, and policy actions to pursue it over time.

•For the Fed, the longer-run objective is 2% PCE inflation, alongside maximum employment.

•Policy can respond to expected inflation and employment conditions before inflation reaches the goal.

Why Policymakers Use Judgment

•Potential output and the neutral real rate are estimates that can change.

•Inflation measures and forecasts can disagree; effects of policy arrive with lags.

•The effective lower bound can limit conventional rate cuts. A formula can prescribe an infeasible rate.

Check Monetary Neutrality

•After a one-time monetary expansion, wages and prices fully adjust. Productive capacity is unchanged. Will real output stay above potential?

•What long-run result would you expect after a one-time monetary contraction under the same conditions?

Explanation. Expansion: real output returns to potential and the price level is higher. Contraction: real output returns to potential and the price level is lower. Short-run output and employment effects can still be substantial.

Policy Reasoning

•Identify the shock and the output gap before recommending a policy.

•Use a rule to organize the evidence; distinguish a benchmark from a binding decision.

•Explain the expected channel, the tradeoff, and the condition that could change your answer.

Explanation. An output gap describes the economy’s position. A policy rule supplies a numerical cross-check. An inflation objective and mandate guide the decision over time. Monetary neutrality describes the long-run result after wages and prices adjust.
