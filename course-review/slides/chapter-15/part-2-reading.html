Inflation Targeting and the Output Gap

IndicatorMeaning
Inflation targetThe inflation rate the central bank aims for over time. The Fed’s longer-run goal is 2% PCE inflation.
Output gapHow far real GDP is above or below potential GDP, as a percentage of potential GDP.
Output gap = 100 × (Y − Y*) / Y*

•If Y = 980 and Y* = 1,000, the output gap is −2%.

•Use inflation and output forecasts to guide policy. The Fed also seeks maximum employment.

The Taylor Rule

r*: neutral real rateπ: inflationπ*: inflation targetg: output gap
2%4%2%−2%
i = r* + π + 0.5(π − π*) + 0.5g

•Start with r* + π = 2 + 4 = 6%.

•Inflation above target adds 1 point; the negative output gap subtracts 1 point.

•Suggested policy rate: 6%. The rule is a benchmark, not an automatic decision.

The Zero Lower Bound

•When the nominal policy rate is near 0%, there is little room for further conventional rate cuts.

•Cash pays 0% interest. Deeply negative rates encourage people to switch to cash.

•A Taylor rule may suggest a rate below zero. Other tools, including QE, can provide support.

The effective lower bound can be slightly below zero.

Quantitative Easing

•QE: the central bank buys large amounts of longer-term bonds, such as Treasuries and mortgage-backed securities.

•The purchases raise demand for those bonds. Their prices rise and their yields fall.

•Lower longer-term borrowing costs can support consumption, investment, and aggregate demand.

•QE can help when short-term rates are near zero. It does not guarantee more bank lending.

•A one-time money-supply change does not permanently change productive capacity.

Monetary Neutrality

•Monetary neutrality: a change in money supply changes nominal variables, but not real output in the long run.

•If money supply rises by 10%, the long-run price level rises by 10%, with real output unchanged.

•More dollars do not create more workers, machines, or technology.

One-time change in money supply; full price adjustment; productive capacity unchanged.

Monetary Neutrality Checkpoint

•After a one-time increase in money supply and full price adjustment, does real output remain above potential? Explain the change in money demand.

Explanation. No. Real output returns to potential. Higher prices raise nominal money demand; the long-run price level is higher.
